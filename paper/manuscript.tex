\section{Introduction}
An adaptive controller should learn to operate under new conditions while preserving behavior acquired earlier. These are separate requirements: a policy can improve on a new condition while deteriorating on an old one, and a training plateau is not the same as forgetting. Our long-term objective is real-time visual control in TrackMania~2020 (Fig.~\ref{fig:workflow}a), where training plateaus with transformer and world-model components motivated a controlled study of how interaction history and retained experience affect continued learning. Primacy bias~\cite{primacy}, loss of plasticity~\cite{plasticity} and forgetting during fine-tuning~\cite{finetuneforget} are relevant mechanisms, but none can be assigned to a run without measuring new-condition and old-condition performance directly. We therefore measure both.

The intervention is a change in \emph{sensing}. A sensor fault alters what the policy observes, whereas an actuator fault alters the applied control; delayed or noisy signals change the information a policy needs~\cite{signaldelay}. History conditioning supplies past observations, actions and rewards; a transformer is one way to encode them, and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)~\cite{sac} supplies the learning objective. In-context RL uses history to adapt with fixed parameters~\cite{rl2,amago,ad}. Our question concerns \emph{continued parameter learning}: after a trained controller encounters changed sensing, does further training improve its return, what happens to its earlier clean-condition performance, and what does mixing earlier clean experience into replay do?

We study this in HalfCheetah-v5 and Ant-v5. Eight source checkpoints per controller and task initialize a history-conditioned transformer and a reactive controller. Each checkpoint branches into two sensor faults and two replay fractions, plus a control that continues training on the clean condition. All branches receive 20,000 new interactions, and the same updated weights are evaluated on the new and the old condition. The study extends an earlier three-seed, 4,000-step pilot, whose branches it reproduces exactly; the statistical analysis was fixed before the runs.

Our contributions are (i) a behaviorally verified sensor-fault intervention for continued SAC training of history-conditioned and reactive controllers; (ii) paired adaptation and retention measurements in two environments, with a control that continues training on clean sensing and with pre-specified paired tests; (iii) a critic-level analysis of replay mixing based on held-out soft Bellman residuals and value bias; and (iv) inference-latency measurements and released code, wrappers, seeds and checkpoints. We do not propose a new algorithm or claim a solution to forgetting.

\subsection{Related Work}
\paragraph{Memory, delay and adaptation.} Recurrent model-free policies are strong baselines under partial observability when carefully tuned~\cite{ni}. Off-policy transformer agents for in-context and multi-task RL are studied in AMAGO and AMAGO-2~\cite{amago,amago2}, and Algorithm Distillation learns from learning histories~\cite{ad}. Signal delay degrades standard deep RL, and delay-aware state augmentation and prediction recover much of the loss~\cite{signaldelay}. We use none of these complete methods; our transformer is a small SAC encoder whose history resets each episode, so cross-episode adaptation happens only through parameter updates.

\paragraph{Replay and retention.} CLEAR combines replay with behavioral cloning for continual RL~\cite{clear}, RLPD uses prior data for efficient online RL~\cite{rlpd}, and fine-tuning pre-trained RL models forgets capabilities in states the new task does not visit early, which knowledge-retention techniques mitigate~\cite{finetuneforget}. Our retained replay is the simplest rehearsal: half of each batch is old clean experience, with no cloning or feature-preservation loss.

\paragraph{Optimization and empirical design.} The controllers use a REDQ-style critic ensemble~\cite{redq}; DroQ and CrossQ show that normalization and update schedules affect efficiency~\cite{droq,crossq}. Benchmark results with few seeds show \emph{that} a method performed well rather than \emph{why}~\cite{benchmarking}, and empirical-design guidance calls for fixed budgets, all seeds and appropriate uncertainty~\cite{empirical}. We follow both by pairing outcomes with controlled contrasts and a mechanism-level critic diagnostic.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Environments and Sensor Faults}
We use the Gymnasium MuJoCo tasks~\cite{gym} HalfCheetah-v5 (17 observation coordinates: 8 positions and 9 velocities; 6 actions; no termination) and Ant-v5 (27 coordinates without the 78 contact forces: 13 positions and 14 velocities; 8 actions; terminates when the torso leaves the healthy height range). Episodes last at most 1,000 steps and actions lie in $[-1,1]$. Condition $A$ uses true observations. Condition $B$ alters only the sensor vector; commands and the base reward are unchanged. For noise, the policy receives
\begin{equation}
 o_t=s_t+\epsilon_t,\qquad \epsilon_{t,j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_j^2),
\end{equation}
independently across coordinates and steps, with $\sigma_j=0.1$ for positions and $0.5$ for velocities in native units, drawn from a generator separate from the simulator's reset randomness. For delay, $o_t=s_{t-3}$, with a zero vector until three earlier readings exist. Both faults start at reset and remain active.

A Gymnasium wrapper implements both faults. At each step it forwards the command unchanged to the simulator, appends the true observation to a four-element FIFO that is cleared at reset, and emits
\begin{equation}
 x_t=[o_t,a_{t-1},r_{t-1},d_{t-1}]\in\mathbb{R}^{25}\ \text{(HalfCheetah)}\ \text{or}\ \mathbb{R}^{37}\ \text{(Ant)},
 \label{eq:input}
\end{equation}
where $a_{t-1}$, $r_{t-1}$ and the termination flag $d_{t-1}$ keep their correct timing and are zero at reset. True readings are logged for audits only. Under delay, $s_t$ is in principle recoverable from $o_t=s_{t-3}$ and the last three commands, because the dynamics are deterministic~\cite{signaldelay}; a policy that sees only $x_t$ has $a_{t-1}$ but not $a_{t-3}$ and $a_{t-2}$.

\subsection{Controllers and History Conditioning}
Table~\ref{tab:arch} summarizes both controllers. The transformer receives the most recent at most 32 tokens of the current episode. Each token passes through a two-layer embedding (Linear, LayerNorm, GELU, Linear; 64 features) plus a learned absolute position embedding, then two pre-norm causal transformer blocks. The output at the newest token, $h_t$, and an 8-dimensional linear projection $z_t$ of it form the 72-dimensional feature used by the actor and by every critic. The context resets at each episode, is recomputed at every step without caching, and carries no information across episodes; only parameter updates do. The reactive controller maps the single token $x_t$ directly to its actor and critics. It therefore sees the previous command and reward but cannot integrate a sequence. The controllers differ in capacity and preprocessing as well as memory, so comparisons between them are controller comparisons, not isolated causal estimates of memory.

The source models for each task are validation-selected checkpoints of training seeds 42--49 from one SAC recipe: 100 iterations, each collecting four episodes under randomly sampled \emph{actuator} perturbations (action scaling and action noise) and then performing 100 actor and temperature updates, each preceded by ten critic-ensemble updates (at most 400,000 steps; 100,000 critic and 10,000 actor updates). Transformer batches hold 64 sequences of 24 steps whose first 8 steps only build context; reactive batches hold 64 transitions. The transformer recipe adds a fault-classification loss (weight 0.05) whose head is unused here. No source trained with sensor faults.

\subsection{Continued Training, Retained Replay and Control}
For each source model, two deterministic clean episodes form a fixed old pool shared by its branches and disjoint from evaluation. Each source model then initializes five branches: noise or delay crossed with old-replay fraction $\rho\in\{0,0.5\}$, and a control that continues training on $A$ with $\rho=0$. The control measures the change caused by continued optimization alone. Each branch collects 20,000 steps with its stochastic policy and performs 0.25 critic-ensemble and actor updates per step (5,000 each) on batches of 64 transitions: all new for $\rho=0$, or 32 old and 32 new for $\rho=0.5$. Collection is step-budgeted, so terminated Ant episodes restart and episodes are cut at evaluation budgets (as time-limit truncations); replay draws episodes in proportion to their length. Paired branches share their adaptation seed and reset schedule. Optimizers and the entropy temperature are re-initialized identically because they were not stored with the sources.

The critic target is $y=r+\gamma(1-d)\big(\min_{j\in S}\bar Q_j(h',a')-\alpha\log\pi(a'|h')\big)$ with $a'\sim\pi(\cdot|h')$, $S$ two random critics of ten and $\gamma=0.99$; critic losses are summed over the ensemble. The actor minimizes $\alpha\log\pi-\min(Q_1,Q_2)$ on detached transformer features, so the representation learns through critic regression. Target networks use Polyak retention 0.995 and dropout is disabled throughout. Each transformer sample trains its final valid transition with the exact rolling history that produced it; one quarter of samples come from the first 32 steps of an episode, so short contexts are represented. Table~\ref{tab:config} lists the settings.

\subsection{Measurements and Statistical Analysis}
At each budget $n$, the same checkpoint is evaluated on $A$ and $B$ with frozen parameters, a fresh context per episode and five fixed environment seeds that never enter replay. For source seed $i$, fault $c$ and fraction $\rho$, the new-condition change is $\Delta^B(n)=R^B(n)-R^B(0)$ and the retention change is $\Delta^A(n)=R^A(n)-R^A(0)$, where $R$ is the mean evaluation return. A negative $\Delta^A$ is a loss of measured clean performance. Checkpoints are fixed by budget; no outcome selects them.

The replication unit is the source seed ($n=8$); evaluation episodes are averaged within a seed first, and branches from one source model are not independent. The analysis was fixed before the runs. The primary family is the paired replay effect $D_i=\text{metric}_i(\rho{=}0.5)-\text{metric}_i(\rho{=}0)$ for $\Delta^A$ and $\Delta^B$ at 20,000 steps, for each controller and fault (eight tests per task), using exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm correction. We report mean differences with 95\% $t$ intervals and seed-bootstrap intervals (10,000 resamples). A secondary family tests fault-specific forgetting, $\Delta^A$ of each $\rho=0$ fault branch minus $\Delta^A$ of the clean control (four tests per task); per-arm changes are tested against zero in the released analysis files. With eight seeds the smallest attainable exact $p$ is 0.0078, so a Holm-adjusted $p$ cannot fall below 0.0625 in the eight-test primary family or below 0.031 in the four-test family. This floor follows from the pre-specified design, and we report it rather than change the correction after the runs: conclusions about the replay effect rest on the interval estimates and on sign agreement across seeds, and non-significance is not evidence of equivalence.

\subsection{Critic Diagnostic for Replay Mixing}\label{sec:critic}
With fraction $\rho$, the critic regresses onto targets drawn from the mixture $(1-\rho)\mathcal{D}_B+\rho\mathcal{D}_A$. Two distortions are possible. Old transitions were generated by the source policy, so their targets rely on the current actor's actions in histories it is not being trained to act in (an off-policy mismatch). Moreover, a history can occur under both sensing channels while implying different states, especially under delay; its regression target is then a $\rho$-weighted blend of two Bellman targets, biasing the fitted value for both. To measure these effects, we evaluate each saved checkpoint's critics on its own held-out evaluation trajectories from $A$ and $B$. We report the root-mean-square soft Bellman residual $y-\frac{1}{10}\sum_j Q_j(h,a)$, computed with the training target and the saved temperature, averaging over all critic pairs, and the Monte-Carlo bias $Q-G_t$ for steps with a complete discounted return $G_t$. The critics estimate the soft value of the stochastic policy whereas $G_t$ is the deterministic policy's return. This offset is shared by the $A$ and $B$ data of one checkpoint, so instead of raw biases we compare the value-bias gap, the bias on $B$ data minus that on $A$ data.

\subsection{Verification, Latency and Reproducibility}
Twelve behavioral tests check corruption, timing, random-stream separation, replay boundaries, masking, parameter updates and positional support. A real MuJoCo test applies identical commands under clean, noisy and delayed sensing and requires identical states and rewards. Every run snapshots its source files, protocol, package versions and checkpoint hashes, and records commands, model inputs, audit-only true readings and rewards for every evaluation. We measure inference latency of the deployment call (a full 32-token forward pass for the transformer) at batch size one with real model inputs, excluding simulation, on CPU with one and four threads and on GPU. Runs use Python 3.12.10, PyTorch 2.11.0 with CUDA 12.8, Gymnasium 1.3.0 and MuJoCo 3.12.0 on Windows with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 Laptop GPU; rollouts infer on CPU and updates run on GPU.
